Small amplitudes can make phase rounding less damaging. They do not make phase information automatically disposable—and modifying a learned bias is very different from modifying an actual key.

This page reports recovered exploratory measurements from “ROPE investigation,” using Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct. All layer and head indices are zero-based. Original figures and numeric outputs are included, with hashes in the provenance manifest. The current blog build replays saved measurements; it does not claim a new full-model replication.

First define the angle and radius

There are several distinct quantities in these experiments:

  • An actual key’s pre-RoPE phase: \(\phi_k=\operatorname{atan2}(k_y,k_x)\), with key radius \(r_k=\sqrt{k_x^2+k_y^2}\).
  • A relative rotated query–key angle, whose cosine sets that pair’s dot-product contribution.
  • A query–key contrast angle for a winner-minus-runner-up margin.
  • The angle of a learned projection bias, or of a residual around a calibrated center.

These angles are not interchangeable. Nor is low radius the same as low frequency. A pair can be slow but large, or fast but small. At near-zero radius its phase is poorly determined, but the same key pair can matter when the corresponding query amplitude is large.

Was there little use of angles beyond π/2?

The broad scarcity claim is not supported. The archived native-margin analysis covers 16 prose passages, 768 query positions, and all 336 query heads: 16,515,072 pair observations. Only 43.80% of pair contributions to the winner–runner-up margin were positive; 57.38% of available amplitude lay in positive-contribution observations. Negative contributions are therefore common in this particular decomposition.

For a signed wrapped effective phase in \([-\pi,\pi]\), the negative-cosine region is \(|\theta|>\pi/2\). On \([0,2\pi)\), “θ>π/2” also includes part of the positive-cosine region, so that threshold alone is ambiguous. More importantly, a winner–runner-up contrast is not the angle between an ordinary query and one key, and an individual key’s pre-RoPE phase does not identify suppression.

The candidates were selected using the full score. Their full summed margin is positive by construction; individual frequency pairs may oppose it. Counts do not measure causal mechanism usage or downstream importance. This evidence corrects the outline’s generalization without pretending that a margin histogram establishes a universal angle distribution.

Phase versus amplitude

The investigation compared \(r<1\), \(1\le r\le5\), and \(r>5\). Small-radius phases are often broadly spread; larger-radius groups can show pronounced peaks. The archive also measures concentration within each head/pair and shuffles the phase-to-amplitude correspondence there. Pooling arbitrary phase origins can hide concentration, so a pooled histogram alone is insufficient.

Actual slow-key phases: 0, 2, and 3 bits

This intervention changes actual k_proj activations, including learned bias, before RoPE. It preserves each selected pair’s radius. Pairs 49–63, all 28 layers, and both shared KV heads are eligible. Four previously used 2,062-token book excerpts provide 7,168 scored next-token predictions. They are not a fresh final test set.

Zero phase bits means mapping a selected pair to \((r,0)\). Two phase bits retain four directions, spaced by π/2. Three retain eight directions, spaced by π/4. None of these tests compresses the radius or measures packed storage and runtime.

{{slow_table}}

For small slow pairs, three bits gave +0.00170 nats/token and 3.19% changed top-one predictions; two bits gave +0.01824 and 7.63%. Collapsing small slow phases to zero gave +0.26280 and 29.30%. Coarse rounding is substantially more tolerable than eliminating phase choice.

Protecting the first 14 keys gave +0.00124 and 3.08% changed predictions for the small-pair three-bit condition. Other protected-prefix results are not uniformly better; the complete conditions remain in the data. “Predictions changed” means disagreement with the baseline, not necessarily incorrectness.

A separate short-context experiment rounded all 64 pairs only when actual \(r<2.5\). Across 16 passages and 2,251 targets, three-bit phase rounding selected 75.71% of pair instances, increased loss by +0.01394 nats/token, and changed 7.69% of predictions. Its saved loss-increase interval was [0.00527, 0.02180]. These results have different scope from the slow-only long-context experiment.

The narrower “almost free” result concerns biases

For slow learned key-bias pairs 49–63, rounding bias phases to π/2 spacing changed no top-one predictions among 2,251 targets, with −0.000022 nats/token mean loss change. Removing the entire small bias vector, \(r<2.5\), changed 0.044% of predictions, with +0.000009 nats/token. Tiny negative changes do not establish improvement.

These are constant projection biases, not the whole token-dependent key. “Remove the vector” also differs from “set its phase to zero.” Neither result supports saying actual slow keys need zero phase bits. See the original bias-rounding and bias-removal results.

Phase rounding is not a complete codec

A centered codec stores a deviation from a calibrated typical pair and adds that center back at decoding. For a key \((10,1)\) with center \((10,0)\), the residual \((0,1)\) has a completely different phase. Coarsening actual origin-based phases and coarsening centered residuals are different operations.

{{codec_table}}

The three-bit shortcut is promising as a phase-only intervention, but the tested full codec did not beat the earlier centered 6-phase/4-radius-bit codec at essentially equal storage. Values remain uncompressed. A reported K-only compression factor is therefore not a full-KV memory reduction, and neither implies a measured speedup.

The independently tested bound explains why radius can help: for a key pair rotated by phase error \(\epsilon\),

\[\|k'-k\|_2=2r_k|\sin(\epsilon/2)|\le r_k|\epsilon|,\qquad |\delta s_j|\le\frac{\|q_j\|_2r_k|\epsilon|}{\sqrt d}.\]

For a small logit perturbation at temperature one, \(\delta o\approx\sum_i p_i\epsilon_i(v_i-o)\). Errors depend jointly on queries, keys, attention mass, and values. Radius alone is not a complete importance metric.

How this relates to published compression work

PolarQuant quantizes angles after random preconditioning and a recursive polar transform. Our raw two-dimensional RoPE-pair phase intervention is a different representation. KIVI quantizes keys per channel and values per token, with a recent residual buffer. Its “2-bit” refers to scalar quantization, not two bits for a pair’s phase. Neither paper independently validates the exact experiments here.
