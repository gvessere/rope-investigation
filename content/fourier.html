A sum of waves sounds promising. But I wanted to see it do something concrete: build a score that rises near a chosen distance, then falls away again.

Imagine that the information we want is around eighty tokens in the past. A single cosine will keep coming back to its maximum. What we want instead is a bump: high near eighty, low on either side.

A Gaussian gives us an easy target to draw. It is not a statement about what a model ought to learn. It is simply a shape we can ask two different sets of waves to approximate.

First, try the familiar Fourier ingredients

The DFT provides a useful starting point. With a complete set of Fourier coefficients, we can reconstruct all the samples of our target. With fewer components, we get an approximation—and can watch what is lost.

What matters here is the idea of cooperation. Each wave extends across the whole window. There is no individual component that says “eighty.” The bump appears because components reinforce each other in one region and cancel elsewhere.

That is the kind of construction I was looking for in RoPE.

Now change the available frequencies

In the comparison below, the black line is the target. One approximation uses a small Fourier basis; the other uses a geometric RoPE frequency schedule. Both get sixteen coefficient slots.

On the left, we can see how well each set of ingredients builds the target. On the right, we ask a different question: what happens where we did not fit it?

The RoPE approximation becomes unstable outside the fitted window. In this example, several of its very slow waves look almost alike over the training interval. The fit gives them large coefficients that cancel there. Beyond the interval, the cancellation breaks down.

I would not read this as “RoPE cannot make a bump,” or as a simulation of a trained head. It shows why the available frequency set matters. Two systems can both add sinusoids and still have quite different approximation behavior.

What was held equal, and what was not?

The target is a Gaussian centered at 80, with width parameter 8. The fit uses 128 samples. The Fourier matrix has DC, seven sine/cosine harmonic pairs, and a Nyquist column. The RoPE matrix has eight frequency pairs, head dimension 16, and base 10,000. Both have sixteen columns, but their frequency coverage and effective rank differ.

The least-squares cutoff is \(10^{-10}\). The RoPE matrix has effective rank 14; the coefficient norm is about 24 million. Its extrapolation RMSE is about 4,935. Those are properties of this deliberately unregularized example, not reported statistics of a trained model. Full numerical results.

Can we construct useful shapes against real keys?

The original investigation went a step closer to a model. We took four key vectors captured from a Qwen prompt, kept them fixed, and constructed query vectors to give the matching key a particular distance response.

Instead of one target shape, we tried four: a small local window, a broad window, two preferred neighborhoods, and a region of suppression.

Here the waves do combine into recognizable shapes. The local window rises around distance fifteen. The broad one spreads its preference over a much larger region. The two-neighborhood example gives us two places to look. Reversing the sign makes a dip rather than a bump.

These queries were constructed by us; they were not the model’s untouched queries. That distinction matters to the question I started with. We have shown a capability of the score family against these keys. We have not yet shown that the model normally uses that capability in this way.

There is another useful detail: the construction keeps the query’s slow component and its overall norm. It changes the fast component to shape the range response. That lets us look at content matching and distance shaping together.

The target can remain the best match even when its full score is negative. The zero line is not a boundary between “attend” and “ignore.” What matters for softmax is the score relative to the other available keys.

And the highest point is not the whole story

At first it is tempting to judge a construction by whether it puts the maximum in the right place. But attention does not stop at finding a maximum. It uses softmax to mix the value vectors:

\[p_i=\frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_k e^{s_k}},\qquad o=\sum_i p_i v_i.\]

A narrow peak and a broad hill can have the same highest score while producing very different mixtures. Two nearly tied keys might both contribute useful information. There is no general reason a head must separate a winner from a runner-up by a large margin.

This changed the question for the next part of the investigation. It was no longer enough to ask what curves we could build. We needed to ask how much the model’s actual output depended on the angles and amplitudes it had learned.

A few details behind the Fourier connection

The complete DFT reconstructs \(N\) samples using \(N\) independent real degrees of freedom for a real-valued sequence. It does not identify a unique underlying continuous function; its usual continuation is periodic. See the DFT definitions.

RoPE’s fixed-content score family has at most two real coefficients per frequency pair. It can fit a sampled target exactly only when that target lies in its basis matrix’s column space. Positional similarity between slow frequencies also does not mean the learned projections carry the same content. Round and Round We Go! explores positional and low-frequency behavior in trained models.
