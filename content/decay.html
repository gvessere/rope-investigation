The wave picture showed that RoPE can create preferences at different distances. Looking at real attention raised a different question: how much attention actually reaches into the past?

One pattern in the investigation was particularly easy to picture. In some heads, the stretch from sixteen to thirty-one tokens back received about as much attention as the stretch from thirty-two to sixty-three. And the next, twice-as-large stretch received a similar amount again.

That suggests a simple tradeoff: further-away tokens get a smaller share individually, while an entire band of distant history can still matter.

Before trusting the pattern, make the comparison fair

There is a trap in pooling attention over a passage. A query near the beginning has no distant history available. If we count distant keys without correcting for that, they can appear unimportant partly because they were not there.

To avoid this, the investigation looked at queries near the end of each passage. Every query in the comparison could see the whole distance range being tested. We then grouped the past into doubling intervals: 8–15, 16–31, and so on up to 512–1023 tokens.

For this head, each doubling interval receives roughly seven to nine percent of the total attention. But the later interval contains twice as many keys. Each key therefore gets about half as much weight, on average.

That is what an approximately \(1/m\) pattern looks like, where \(m\) is age in tokens. Double the age, halve the average weight per key.

One small calculation makes the picture click

Imagine giving the same attention budget to every equal step in logarithmic distance. Moving from 8 to 16 is one such step; so is moving from 16 to 32. Converting that budget back into ordinary distance gives

\[w(m)\,\mathrm dm=C\,\mathrm d(\log m)=\frac C m\,\mathrm dm.\]

So \(w(m)=C/m\). The inverse-distance shape follows from equal mass per logarithmic interval. For integer-token bins the equality is approximate, but the intuition survives: twice as many keys share the next band’s budget.

It is an appealing explanation of the plot. It also leaves a question open: why should the head allocate its attention that way in the first place?

Was this one head, or a general pattern?

We checked all the query heads. Roughly thirty percent of the eligible heads met the investigation’s descriptive criterion for approximate inverse-distance behavior in their averaged curves. Many did not.

The first example is useful because it makes the idea visible. The full map keeps us from treating it as the behavior of every head. Even an averaged head curve can hide substantial changes between queries.

The measurement behind “roughly thirty percent”

These are Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct measurements on four roughly 2k-token excerpts, using the final 128 scored queries per excerpt. The fitted ages are 8–1023 in seven complete doubling bins. Bin mass is divided by available key count before a log-log fit.

The descriptive criterion was exponent 0.8–1.2, log-space R² at least 0.8, positive mass in every bin, and at least 1% of total attention in the range. 101 of 333 eligible dense heads matched; 46 matched separately in all four texts. The fraction among individual eligible query/head cases was 15.82%. These are not formal tests distinguishing power laws from all alternative distributions.

L2 H5 has pooled exponent 1.0054 and R²=0.9968, with 56.73% of its attention in the fitted range. It was chosen after the scan as an illustration. Protocol and results · All head fits.

Does RoPE’s frequency schedule explain it?

This brings us back to the geometric ladder of frequencies. Each doubling of distance takes us through another set of scales. It is tempting to connect that ladder directly to equal attention per doubling interval.

There is a plausible story: rapidly rotating terms increasingly cancel when averaged, while slower terms remain aligned. If each doubling produces a similar score penalty, scores could decrease roughly with the logarithm of distance.

Softmax would then turn that logarithmic score into a power law:

\[s(m)=c-\lambda\log m\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(m)\propto m^{-\lambda}.\]

But the “if” is doing real work. The coefficients, content, and averaging have to produce that score shape. RoPE’s geometric frequencies alone do not force them to do so. We have already constructed score curves that rise at a chosen distance rather than simply decaying.

Round and Round We Go! makes this objection to universal decay explanations. Other analyses, including Loveland and colleagues’ work on graph reasoning, derive decay under additional alignment assumptions. Those assumptions are part of the explanation.

Where the maximum-entropy argument fits

There is another conditional route to a power law. On a finite range of positive ages, fix the average log-age and choose the distribution with maximum Shannon entropy. Lagrange multipliers give

\[p_m=\frac{m^{-\lambda}}{\sum_{r=1}^M r^{-\lambda}}.\]

The average-log-age constraint determines \(\lambda\); it does not make it one automatically. At one, normalization is \(1/(mH_M)\). An exact inverse-distance distribution cannot be normalized on an infinite positive-integer range.

The author’s discussion of attention decay describes the log-distance constraint as a hypothesis. The Zenodo record cited in the earlier chat could not be retrieved during verification. Neither the constraint nor entropy maximization has been established as the training mechanism in these Qwen experiments.

What I take away from the exploration

I started by asking whether RoPE could do more than favor nearby tokens. Following the rotations into a sum of waves made it possible to construct much richer score shapes. Looking at actual keys then showed that a small vector can tolerate a rough direction without making its phase irrelevant.

The distance measurements added another perspective: some heads distribute attention across scales, while others behave differently. There is a connection worth investigating between those scales and RoPE’s frequency schedule, but the plots do not close the explanation by themselves.

That leaves a more specific next question: can we change the relevant frequency contributions, preserve the content comparison, and make the measured decay change in the predicted way? That would move us from recognizing a pattern toward explaining it.
